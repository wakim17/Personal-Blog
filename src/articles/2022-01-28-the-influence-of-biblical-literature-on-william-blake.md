# Introduction

**How does William Blake convey the religious conditioning of children in the poems The Chimney Sweeper and The Little Black Boy?**

William Blake: “The Bible is the greatest work of poetry ever written" (Barker, 2021). In his lifetime he was a poet, painter, engraver, and philosopher. Many scholars would believe he was beyond the romantic age and unique in his place in history. Young Blake was baptised almost a month after his birth on 11th December. The Bible became a significant text that inspired him throughout his life. His interest bloomed in poetry, as it came from the reading of Ben Jonson, Edmund Spenser, and the book of Psalms. (Wilson, 1927). 

In August 1772, Blake became an apprentice for James Basire, an engraver. At the age of 21, he was regarded as a professional engraver. He began to create his own unique designs and ideas. He is said to have had visions there, specifically visions of Christ and his apostles (Wilson, 1927). These visions would inspire and create a framework for his engravings and poetry throughout his life. Blake printed his first poetry collection, ‘Poetical Sketches’. Blake and his friend James Parker opened a print shop in the following year. Blake had a technique to produce his text by constructing on copper plates with an impervious liquid. Then the plate is dipped in acid, so the text and design get imprinted on the plate. That plate would be used for printing on paper, and the final copy would be hand coloured. In 1788, Blake used his technique for There is No Natural Religion and All Religions are One; this led to the development of plates for poems in the collection, Songs of Innocence, in 1789. In 1794, the volume was added to the "Song of Experience". 

Blake was writing poems that focused on humanity’s collapse from grace. “The Torments of Love and Jealousy in the Death and Judgement of Albion the Ancient Man". It represents humanity and obviously indicates that Blake enjoyed merging religious commentary with politics. (Poetry Foundation, 2021)  

The poem Jerusalem used around a hundred illustrated plates. It took Blake 16 years to fully complete. According to the Poetry Foundation, “Blake develops his mythology to explore man’s fall and salvation. As the narrative begins in Jerusalem, man is apart from God and split into several identities. As the poem develops, man’s split identities slowly integrate, and man is unified with the divinity that is within him” (Poetry Foundation, 2021). 

William Blake was admired by the world over for his enthusiastic, revolutionary, and inspired visions of illustrating the art and written word. By some, he was called a saint, and by some, a sinner. Blake changed art and poetry forever, and his works will never be forgotten.

Biblical literature excels in time. No matter what one’s religious beliefs are, its contents have had one of the greatest influences on humanity since its beginning. A part of the Bible, the Old Testament is a collection of writings that was preserved as the sacred books of the Hebrew people. The other part of the Bible, the New Testament, is the writing that narrates the origin and dissemination of Christianity. Biblical literature, incorporating the Old and New Testaments, has played an important role in history and culture and has itself become a massive subject of intensive critical study. “The Bible is the literature of faith, not scientific observation or historical demonstration.” (Biblical literature | Britannica, 2021).

The Bible influenced famous poets and writers such as Shakespeare and Milton. Paradise Lost and other major poems by Milton dramatise stories from the Bible. The Bible was a vital source of inspiration and criticism for William Blake. Christopher Rowland is an English theologist who argues that Blake's approach to the Bible was generally consistent, even though he suffered from a religious change in his later years. Rowland also illustrates how Blake saw himself as a prophet and somehow saw himself continuing the work of John, the author of the Book of Revelation. 

The two poems that I will be comparing are The Chimney Sweeper and The Little Black Boy, both by Blake, which both illustrate the miserable lives of children. At the end of these poems, it teaches us how God will always reward a being suffering and hard work by granting access to eternity in heaven. Personally, these poems have significance to my life. These poems could be relatable to other struggling children, where they will find God, and their perspective will be changed.

## The Chimney Sweeper 

The Chimney Sweeper is a popular poem with the theme of poverty, misery, death, and hope from the perspective of the life of children in labour. The poem consists of the agony of children who lived a miserable life. The children had to survive by sweeping chimneys at a very young age during the time of William Blake. The speaker describes how he gets involved in the sweeping chimney business. He says that his father had put him to work after the death of his mother. The speaker also describes the story of his companion chimney sweeper, Tom, who was injured when his head was shaved. Tom then had a dream in which he saw all the sweepers in coffins, and an angel came and set the children free. Then they play joyfully in the sun, and the angel tells Tom that he will get to heaven of his own accord. The next morning when he wakes up, he decides to work harder because he now believes that if he works hard, he will get rewarded.

The poem opens with the speaker notifying us that his mother died when he ‘was very young’. This line is just an informative opener and hooks the reader very effectively.

> *“When my mother died, I was very young.*
>
> *And my father sold me while yet my tongue*
>
> *Could scarcely cry ‘weep! ‘weep! ‘weep! ‘weep!*
>
> *So your chimneys I sweep & in soot, I sleep”*

The skilful use of assonance is a literary device in which the repetition of rhyming vowel sounds takes place in two or more words in proximity to each other within a line.” ‘Weep! Weep! Weep! Weep!' is used to describe the pain the speaker felt after the death of the mother. Confessing how his father sold him when he couldn't even speak suggests that he was a toddler at the time. The phrase "my tongue / Could scarcely cry" is a poetic way of saying “before I could even cry."

Blake is using a device called metonymy here. When he says ‘tongue’, he's referring to the speaker's voice, as a tongue cannot make a sound all on its own. In the later eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, most chimney sweepers were young boys, because they were small and could crawl up there with ease. Here the boy's father sold him to a chimney cleaning business. He tells us that because his father sold him, he sweeps chimneys and sleeps in filthy environments. It does not sound pleasant. Sometimes, chimney sweepers would sleep under the blankets or clothes they used to collect soot during the day. In this poem, a snoot symbolises sins, corrupting life and polluting it. As ‘soot’ infects children’s external bodies but not the inner soul.

> *“There’s little Tom Dacre, who cried when his head*
>
> *That curled like a lamb’s back, was shaved, so I said,*
>
> *Hush, Tom! never mind it, for when your head’s bare,*
>
> *You know that the soot cannot spoil your white hair.”*

The second stanza presents Tom Dacre, a fellow chimney sweep who contrasts the speaker. Tom is saddened about his life, so the speaker comforts him until he falls asleep. In this stanza, Blake uses symbolism to symbolise religion. The word “lamb” compares a child's hair with the hair on the back of the lamb. The lamb here symbolises innocence and symbolises John 1:29, “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (Bible.com, 2016). Here Christ is like a lamb, as he was destined to be offered to God upon the cross for the sins of the world. In the same stanza, the speaker tells Tom that “the soot cannot spoil your white hair”. Here white hair symbolises purity, innocence, and cleanliness. In the Bible, Revelation 1:14-15 (Bible.com, 2022) says that Jesus’ hair "was white as white wool, white as snow”. Which also symbolises innocence and purity. The poem Snoot, which symbolises sin, cannot spoil Tom due to his purity.

The following three stanzas verify Tom Dacre’s dream of the chimney sweepers’ "heaven". However, in the final stanza, Tom is found waking up the following morning, with him and the speaker still imprisoned in their treacherous line of work.

> *“That thousands of sweepers, Dick, Joe, Ned, & Jack,* 
>
> *Were all of them locked up in coffins of black?"*

The powerful use of symbolism here signifies black coffins are places where the “thousands of sweepers” are stuck due to their sins. The colour black is associated with gloom, darkness, and evil. Tom Dacre is comforted by the promise of a future outside the “coffin” that is his life’s lot. His present state is terrible and only made bearable by the double-edged hope of a happy afterlife following a quick death. Here, Blake mentions not just the shameful conditions of the children sold into labour, but also the society and its religious aspect that would offer these children palliatives rather than aid. 

> *“And by came an Angel who had a bright key,*
>
> *And he opened the coffins & set them all free."*

This line includes symbolism of God as a means of salvation. ‘Bright key’ is a symbol of the mercy of God, which frees sinful souls from their darkness and then brings them to light and freedom. As 'bright' is a word that carries hope, and the key contains the description of freedom.

“And washing a river and shining in the sun." 

‘Wash in a river’ reminds the readers of the many biblical images of healing and new life that are associated with rivers. With poor sanitation and no running water, washing in a river represents a thorough clean as well as evoking a ministerial paradise. These biblical images are represented in Matthew 3:13–17 (Bible.com, 2022), where Jesus got baptised in the river of Jordan. This line also illustrates the Christian symbolism of baptism as it memorialises the death of the old way of life and rising to a new eternal life.

> *“And the Angel told Tom if he’d be a good boy,*
>
> *He’d have God for his father & never want joy.”*

Here, the poet refers to the moment when the boy was having a moment of peace, disconnected for a while from the real and chaotic world. I can interpret it as if in his dreams he has the hope of salvation and that things will get better soon. Here we can see the presence of religious faith, which means the belief and trust in God. In the penultimate stanza of the poem, I can find a parable containing a final moral, which tries to teach the readers that if we are good people, then God will save us.

> *“Though the morning was cold, Tom was happy & warm;*
>
> *So if all do their duty, they need not fear harm.”*

In the last stanza, the following day of Tom’s dream, he woke up, and even though the weather was ‘cold’, he went to work as a chimney sweeper very ecstatically because of the dream from the previous night. In conclusion, Tom says that if all did their work properly and carefully, they would not need to worry about the possible damage they suffered through. Because well-done work is always congratulated. Referring to the kind of language of the poem, we can say that it is referring to God’s salvation. 

"The Chimney Sweeper" proposes that religion sometimes contributes to children's suffering because the angel told Tom to work hard at his dreadful job so he can earn God's divine love. Does that sound fair? To the miserable chimney-sweepers, it is. Blake was trying to tell the reader that God will treat them with eternity in heaven if they put their trust in God.

## The Little Black Boy 

The Little Black Boy, about racial equality, explains how your earthly identity is temporary and that everyone is worthy of God's divine love. This is narrated through the voice of a “little Black boy", narrating the story of how he came to know his own identity and God. The boy, who was born in the south of Africa, first clarifies that even though he is black-skinned, his soul is as white as that of an English child. He relates how his mother taught him about God, who illuminates light and gives life to all creation and provides comfort and joy to men. “We are put on earth,” says his mother, to learn to accept God’s love. This lesson is carried on to an English child by the Black boy. He vows that when they are both free of their bodies and gratifying in the presence of God, he will shade his white friend until he learns to endure God’s love. Like in The Chimney Sweeper here, the mother reassures the Black boy to recognise that they will soon meet their good fortune with God. Major themes in “The Little Black Boy” are equality, racism, and God’s love. The poem reflects the mother’s thoughts about how she makes her son hopeful about the future. 

> *“my mother bore me in the southern wild,*
>
> *And I am black, but O! my soul is white."*

The words 'southern wild' in the first line of the first stanza imply that the speaker and his mother originate in the south. Blake uses a powerful metaphor to describe one’s purity. “My soul is white” illustrates that even though he is Black, his soul is ‘white’. In the Bible, white is a symbol of innocence, purity, and cleanliness. In the Bible, the word ‘black’ is associated with suffering and death. 

> *“White as an angel is the English child:*
>
> *But I am black as if Bereav’d of light.”* 

This phrase implies that the ‘light’ was taken away from the boy. The alliteration associates dark skin with the state of being "bereav'd of light," The “light” parallels the inner self that is revealed through his soul, not his skin. The speaker is illustrating that his dark skin appears to have not received God’s nourishment, warmth, and love. Similarly, the word "but" is usually used to signal the introduction of a problem. The poem tries to suggest that race is irrelevant in the afterlife and in the eyes of God; it also subconsciously reinforces the idea that being ‘Black’ represents a kind of flaw or imperfection.

> *“My mother taught me underneath a tree."*

William Blake, in this poem, describes a “tree” as the tree of knowledge of good and evil in Paradise; it was the tree that bears the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden. The Bible states, 'The Lord God made all kinds of trees grow out of the ground … trees that were pleasing to the eye and good for food. In the middle of the garden was the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.” (Genesis 2:9). Here the mother is teaching the speaker under the ‘tree’ of knowledge; we can assume the mother is being realistic to the speaker by comforting him and making him understand that they will soon meet their good fortune in the future.

> *“And, pointing to the east, began to say / Look on the rising sun: there God does live"*

The word ‘east’ in the Bible. “Now the LORD God had planted a garden in the east, in Eden; and there he put the man he had formed” (Genesis 2:8), illustrating the direction of the rising sun, which is also associated with the direction where heaven lies. The words “sun” and “God” are words that have different meanings, but the author of the poem wants to deliver a meaning wherein the sun is God who lives. It can be interpreted as if the sun were the kingdom of God.

> *“And these black bodies and this sun-burnt face”* 

In this line Blake powerfully uses imagery. It is said by the Black boy that if Black skin is due to sunburn, it can be interpreted as if the slaves have been working under the scorching sun. Then, he tells us his skin colour is just a cloud, as well as white children's. The child said that he would serve or protect the white boy until he could bear the heat that God had given them. Slavery could be interpreted to show that the whites made the blacks slaves because they could not bear the burden of their own lives. 

The black bodies and sunburnt faces can be seen by our eyes. The words 'underneath a tree' and 'east' are used to show the condition of the situation of the boy and his mother. The words “black bodies” and “sunburnt face” can be interpreted if the little Black boy is near God because his mother said that in the sun, where God lives.

> *“Is but a cloud, and like a shady grove.”* 

A cloud means a soul. He is told that his black skin “is but a cloud” that will be dissipated when his soul meets God in heaven. The black boy conveys this lesson to the English child, explaining that his white skin is also a ‘cloud’. He vows to hide his white friends when they are both freed from their bodies and feel joy in front of God until he also learns to endure the heat of God's love. 

> *“And round my golden tent like lambs rejoice”* 

Blake successfully explains how racial differences disappear in heaven, where the two boys will finally become free and equal and “like lambs rejoice”. The ‘golden tent’ in this poem means the golden tent where God lives in heaven, the house of God. In the Bible, God lives in heaven. The ‘lambs’ have a meaning as the innocence and humility of the boys. In another connotation, it can be interpreted as Jesus because, in the Bible, it is said that Jesus is the lamb of God. “The next day John saw Jesus coming toward him and said, “Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29)

> *‘I say to little English boy; / When I from black and he from white cloud free”*

Here the speaker states that because after death skin colour is irrelevant, he will turn into a cloud, which will be the same colour as the white boy. “And be like him, and he will then love me.” In heaven, the white boy and the black boy will be connected because the barriers of society will be lifted, and they will be free. As they will clearly be viewed the same.

Blake proposes this as a vision of equality and blissful unity. However, the readers never come to know of the White boy's response. Blake kept it as a cliffhanger where we don’t know the choice the little boy had made. This is powerful since the Bible states in Deuteronomy 30:15-18. 

“I have set before you today life and good, death and evil. 16If you obey the commandments of the Lord your God…you shall live and multiply."/"17But if your heart turns away, and you will not hear…18I declare to you today that you shall surely perish. You shall not live long in the land that you are going over the Jordan to enter and possess.” (Bible.com, 2016) Here, it states that God gives you a choice to obey him and live eternal life in heaven or deny God's love and perish. This biblical ideology links with the white boy due to the readers not getting a response at the end. It should only signify that his route is his choice and God can only deal with it.

## Conclusion: Link to other poems and develop

In these poems, Blake resembles the angel (from the chimney sweep) and the mother (from the little black boy) because, in the poems, they are spreading the word of God to these children, and Blake is spreading the gospel or the word via these poems. In the chimney sweeper, the angel has a 'key' to release the speaker and his friends. From the experience the children have with the Angel, Blake was using the Angel to represent the religious position taken by the established church, saying, 'Forget about your pain', 'be a good boy’, 'God will reward you later.' Could Angel's key be church policies that soothe the conscience of the believers? The Little Black Boy tells the story of a boy who is Black. The author writes a conclusion if this poem has a purpose to satirise slavery in those days when white men used Black people as objects of slavery. This poem has meaning as well as the election of a very beautiful word. William Blake delivers his view of religion in this poem to criticise the slave trade in Britain. It can be found in each line of its stanza that the poem gives us meaning about God and Christianity. Children can be trapped in many ways: by poverty, by the abandonment of their families, by living in this mortal flesh, by a church whose principles supported oppression, or due to racism. William Blake gives us meaning in these poems, no matter what you are. If black skin or white skin, poor or rich, is the same in God’s eyes, those physical characteristics are relevant in the afterlife, and all deserve God’s divine love. In both poems, Blake effectively uses a plethora of powerful literary devices to represent the message that God brings in the Bible. He uses them to show how the Bible or God influenced these children in their miserable lives to try to relate to the readers.
